Here are two men. Both believed powerful people were hurting the helpless in secret. Both felt they had to act.

## The first man

In 1966, Peter Buxtun worked for the U.S. Public Health Service in San Francisco. He heard about a study in Macon County, Alabama, and asked to read the reports.

What he found was hard to believe:

- The study had been running since **1932**.
- Hundreds of poor Black men with syphilis were told they were being treated for "bad blood."
- They were not being treated at all.
- When penicillin became the standard cure in the 1940s, they did not get it.

His bosses told him he did not understand the science. He kept pushing for six years. In **July 1972**, his papers reached a reporter. Her story ran, and within months the study was shut down.

He was right.

## The second man

In **December 2016**, a man named Edgar Maddison Welch drove to a pizza place in Washington, D.C., with a rifle. Online stories said children were being held in its basement.

He searched the building. He fired the rifle into a locked door. Behind it was computer equipment. There was no basement. There were no children.

He was wrong.

## The question in the middle

So how do you tell them apart? Both men saw a plot. Both were sure. One stopped a real crime. The other put real people in danger.

That question is what my new book is about. It starts with the fire of ancient Rome and walks all the way to today. You will see how the words "conspiracy theory" were born, which cover-ups turned out to be true, and why our brains are so good at seeing patterns, even ones that are not there.
